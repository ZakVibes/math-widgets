Quantum Field on a Lattice

Each pixel is the value of a field φ at one point of a grid. Horizontal is space; vertical is imaginary time, so the whole picture is a snapshot of the quantum vacuum's fluctuations, shaken by a live Monte Carlo simulation. Drag κ up and watch the field pick a sign, blue or orange, everywhere: that is spontaneous symmetry breaking (Z₂), just like the Ising model.

Now φ has two components, so at every site it is an arrow: its phase is the colour, its length the brightness. The action is unchanged by turning every arrow by the same angle (a U(1) symmetry). In two dimensions that symmetry can never break, which is why there is no sign to pick here; instead the arrows can swirl around points called vortices, and the order is destroyed by their unbinding (the BKT transition).

Things to try
  1. Break the symmetry. Press Randomize, then Broken: blue and orange domains grow until one sign wins. Back to Symmetric and the order melts.
  2. Find the critical point. Press Critical: domains of every size appear, and the mass m falls towards zero (correlations reach across the whole grid).
  3. Watch kinks. Switch to Domain walls at Critical: lines running up the picture are kinks moving through time, small loops are kink–antikink pairs.
  4. Tunnel. Choose Thin strip: the whole of space flips between the two vacua. Press Scan widths to measure the kink mass from how fast tunnelling dies out as space gets wider.
  5. Make it complex. Choose Complex φ and the Vortices view. Go from Vortex gas to Quasi-ordered: free vortices pair up and the stiffness Υ jumps past 2/π. That is the BKT transition.

How to read it
  • pale colour: which vacuum the field is in (blue is −, orange is +).
  • A dark line is a wall between the two vacua (a kink).
  • A line running up the picture is a kink moving through time.
  • A closed loop is a kink–antikink pair popping in and out of the vacuum.
  • A line right across is the whole of space tunnelling at once.
How to read it
  • Faded colours: the phase of φ (see the wheel below).
  • A swirl of colour around a point, every hue appearing once on a small loop, is a vortex. A dark dot marks one that winds counter-clockwise (+), a light dot with a dark rim one that winds clockwise (−, an antivortex).
  • Below the transition, vortices only appear in tightly bound + / − pairs, close enough that from afar their swirls cancel.
  • Above it the pairs unbind into a free gas, and the free vortices scramble the phase over long distances. That is the BKT transition.

Coleman–Mermin–Wagner: in 1+1D a continuous symmetry can never truly break. ⟨|m|⟩ only looks nonzero because the grid is finite, and it shrinks slowly (like N−η/2) as the grid grows; try 64 → 128 → 256.

The correlation length is comparable to the grid: near the critical point, try a bigger grid.

In the ordered phase, the lightest thing a nudge of φ can create is a kink–antikink pair (a kink is a wall between the + and − vacua), and pairs fade out more slowly than a single particle would. So here the number is only a rough decay rate: an overestimate of twice the kink mass.

Correlation along time

On a log plot a straight-ish fall-off means correlations die off like e−mτ, and the slope is the particle mass; it goes to zero at the critical point. Points show the measured correlation with error bars, the line is the fit.

Correlation along an axis

G(r) = ⟨φ(x)·φ(x + r)⟩, averaged over every site and both axes, on log-log axes: a straight line is a power law r−η (on this periodic grid the fit curve bends over slightly towards r = N/2, because r is really a chord on a ring). Points have error bars; the curve is the fit.

Histogram of φ

Averaging over 8×8 blocks smooths out the small wiggles: one hump means no preferred sign, two humps mean the field has chosen a side (or both, in different domains). Single sites always look two-humped at λ = 1, because the well itself has two valleys.

⟨|φ|⟩ is the absolute value of the grid-wide average of φ, smoothed over recent sweeps. It is near 0 when domains of both signs cancel and near the well minimum when one sign wins.

Histogram of the phase angle

The phase angle of every 8×8 block average, averaged over the last ~100 steps. It should be flat: the circle of vacua has no preferred direction, because the field keeps wandering around it. The dashed line is perfectly flat.
