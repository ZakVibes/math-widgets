Quantum Field on a Lattice

Each pixel is the value of a field φ at one point of a grid. Horizontal is space; vertical is imaginary time, so the whole picture is a snapshot of the quantum vacuum's fluctuations, shaken by a live Monte Carlo simulation. Drag κ up and watch the field pick a sign, blue or orange, everywhere: that is spontaneous symmetry breaking (Z₂), just like the Ising model. Tap the picture to place a source that pulls on φ there, then switch to the Average view to see the cloud it makes.

Now φ has two components, so at every site it is an arrow: its phase is the colour, its length the brightness. The action is unchanged by turning every arrow by the same angle (a U(1) symmetry). In two dimensions that symmetry can never break, which is why there is no sign to pick here; instead the arrows can swirl around points called vortices, and the order is destroyed by their unbinding (the BKT transition).

Now every site holds an arrow of fixed length that can point anywhere in 3D, like a compass needle on a ball. Colour shows where it points: hue is the bearing, pale means up, dark means down. Neighbours want to line up (the knob β), but with only one dimension of space (plus time) the order always melts at long range: the correlation length stays finite and a mass appears although the action has none. The arrows can also wrap the ball once around a point, an instanton; the number of wrappings, Q, is always a whole number.

Nothing is random here, and the vertical direction is real time, not Euclidean time: we are not looking at quantum fluctuations any more, but at one classical field φ obeying a wave equation and moving, like a film. Space runs across, time runs up. The field has the same double well as before, with two valleys, φ = −1 (blue) and φ = +1 (orange). A kink is a wall between the valleys, and it behaves like a particle: it has a mass and a speed. Here a kink and an antikink rush at each other. They cannot pass through one another, because far to the left and far to the right the field is stuck in the blue valley, so they can only bounce or stick. Each kink can also wobble (physicists call it its shape mode), and in the crash some of the energy is stored in the wobble and may be handed back. If it is handed back at just the right moment the pair gets out after bouncing twice. That resonance was found by Campbell, Schonfeld and Wingate in 1983. Below about 0.26 times the speed of light most crashes end in a trapped wobbling lump that slowly gives off waves (a bion).

Here you are the source. The field is classical again and moves in real time: space runs across, time runs up, and the newest moment is at the top. It starts at rest in the blue valley, φ = −1. Press and hold anywhere on the picture to push on the field there. In the equation that push is a source term J, an outside force acting on φ. A quick tap only sends out ripples. In the quantum theory those ripples come in lumps called mesons, the field's particles (mass 2), and they always travel slower than light (the dashed lines). Hold longer and the field gets shoved over the hump into the orange valley: a kink and an antikink are born and fly apart. The energy has to come from your finger, and a pair costs at least twice the kink mass, 2 × 4/3 ≈ 2.67.

Things to try
  1. Break the symmetry. Press Broken: nearly the whole grid takes one sign, with small bubbles of the other. Back to Symmetric and both signs mix again. Now turn off Cluster updates and press Randomize at Broken: the slowly growing domains are the computer settling down, not physics. Turn clusters back on and it settles at once.
  2. Find the critical point. Press Critical: domains of every size appear, and the mass m falls towards zero (correlations reach across the whole grid).
  3. Watch kinks. Switch to Domain walls at Critical: lines running up the picture are kinks moving through time, small loops are kink–antikink pairs.
  4. See the mass. At Symmetric, tap the middle of the picture to place a source and switch to the Average view: a small round cloud appears around it. Its size is 1/m. Drag κ towards Critical and watch the cloud swell. The panel "Around your source" measures m from the cloud; compare it with the m from the correlation plot.
  5. Tunnel. Choose Thin strip: the whole of space flips between the two vacua. Press Scan widths to measure the kink mass from how fast tunnelling dies out as space gets wider.
  6. Make it complex. Choose Complex φ and the Vortices view. Go from Vortex gas to Quasi-ordered: free vortices pair up and the stiffness Υ jumps past 2/π. That is the BKT transition.
  7. Wrap the ball. Choose Arrows (O(3)) and the Topology view, press Add instanton, then Run: the hand-made blob is not a typical sample, and the next samples no longer show it. Press Randomize, wait for the picture to settle, then Cool: the noise is stripped away and the instantons (red) and anti-instantons (blue) that remain are left standing. Then compare the Strong, Medium and Weak coupling presets: ξ grows exponentially with β.
  8. Watch a collision in real time. Choose Kink collisions and press Two bounces: the kinks meet, bounce, fall back for a second hit and only then fly apart. Compare One bounce and Trapped (bion), then press Scan speeds to map every speed from 0.15 to 0.30 and click any bar to replay that collision.
  9. Make particles yourself. Choose Poke the field. Tap the picture quickly: only ripples. Now press and hold for about half a second: a kink–antikink pair is born and flies apart. With Gentle strength no pair ever forms, however long you hold, because the push is too weak to lift the field over the hump.

How to read it
  • pale colour: which vacuum the field is in (blue is −, orange is +).
  • A dark line is a wall between the two vacua (a kink).
  • A line running up the picture is a kink moving through time.
  • A closed loop is a kink–antikink pair popping in and out of the vacuum.
  • A line right across is the whole of space tunnelling at once.
How to read it
  • Faded colours: the phase of φ (see the wheel below).
  • A swirl of colour around a point, every hue appearing once on a small loop, is a vortex. A dark dot marks one that winds counter-clockwise (+), a light dot with a dark rim one that winds clockwise (−, an antivortex).
  • Below the transition, vortices only appear in tightly bound + / − pairs, close enough that from afar their swirls cancel.
  • Above it the pairs unbind into a free gas, and the free vortices scramble the phase over long distances. That is the BKT transition.
How to read it
  • Faded colours: which way the arrows point (see the ball below).
  • red marks places where the arrows wrap the ball in the positive sense (charge density q > 0), blue the negative sense (q < 0). Add up all q over the grid and you get Q, always a whole number.
  • A compact red blob adding up to +1 is an instanton: the arrows inside point in every direction exactly once. A blue one is an anti-instanton (−1).
  • In hot noise you see a fine speckle of red and blue that cancels in pairs. Cool melts the speckle and leaves the instantons.
How to read it
  • blue is the − valley (φ = −1), orange the + valley (φ = +1). The pale line between them is a wall (φ = 0): a kink or an antikink.
  • Space runs across and real time runs up, newest moment at the top. A line leaning to the right is a wall moving to the right.

A source adds −J φ to the action at one site, so φ there is pulled one way, and its neighbours follow, less and less with distance. Averaged over many steps, the pull fades like e−m r (more exactly the Bessel function K₀(m r), the propagator of a particle of mass m in two dimensions). So the size of the cloud is the correlation length ξ = 1/m, and the fade-out gives the mass a second way. The cloud is round although one direction is space and the other is time: in Euclidean time the two are on an equal footing. Points: the average of φ at distance r, minus its average outside the measuring circle (radius up to 40 sites); line: the fit over the filled points.

In the ordered phase a source decides which vacuum the whole grid sits in: the field turns to agree with it (a tiny push is enough to pick one of two equal vacua). The particle here is heavy, so its cloud is about one site across, too small to measure. While a source is in, the simulation also refuses the rare jumps of the whole grid to the other vacuum: an infinitely large grid never makes them, but this finite one would, and the picture would flicker.

Asymptotic freedom

Correlation length ξ against β on a log scale. Dots: our own measurements (second-moment ξ₂, Monte Carlo on large grids, error bars smaller than the dots where not visible). Curve: the two-loop asymptotic-scaling formula ξ = C e2πβ/(2πβ), with C fitted to the largest-β dot. Ring: your simulation right now.

The mass appears although the action has none: ξ is finite at every β. And as β grows (weaker coupling, shorter distances) ξ grows exponentially, so the continuum limit sits at β → ∞, where the coupling vanishes. That is asymptotic freedom, the same behaviour that makes quarks nearly free at short distances in QCD.

Correlation along time

On a log plot a straight-ish fall-off means correlations die off like e−mτ, and the slope is the particle mass; it goes to zero at the critical point. Points show the measured correlation with error bars, the line is the fit.

Histogram of φ

Averaging over 8×8 blocks smooths out the small wiggles: one hump means no preferred sign, two humps mean the field has chosen a side (or both, in different domains). Single sites always look two-humped at λ = 1, because the well itself has two valleys.

⟨|φ|⟩ is the absolute value of the grid-wide average of φ, smoothed over recent sweeps. It is near 0 when domains of both signs cancel and near the well minimum when one sign wins.

Histogram of the phase angle

The phase angle of every 8×8 block average, averaged over the last ~100 steps. It should be flat: the circle of vacua has no preferred direction, because the field keeps wandering around it. The dashed line is perfectly flat.

Topological charge, sample by sample

Q in each recent sample (about one per update step), in the order the computer drew them: this axis is not time. Q is always a whole number, so the line is a staircase. At weak coupling it changes rarely from one sample to the next, because the small local updates have to shrink an instanton to a point before they can unwrap it (lattice physicists call this topological freezing; it is a limit of the method, not of nature).
