How to read it

Bodies A and B start one unit apart. Body C starts at whichever point you pick. All three begin at rest and fall together under Newtonian gravity (G = 1). Sooner or later one body is flung away and the other two leave as a bound pair.

Every pixel is its own copy of that system. In the time-lapse, all of them run together on your GPU and are recoloured every frame from their live state. Nearby pixels start almost the same, so at first the picture is smooth. Chaos shows up as the field stretching and folding until neighbouring pixels have nothing in common.

The escape map shows only the ending: which body gets thrown out. Where colours keep switching at every zoom level, a tiny shift in where C starts changes the outcome.

Stretching measures how quickly neighbouring starts are being pulled apart. Its bright ridges are where the fate of the system changes from one pixel to the next.

Tap anywhere to launch that start and four neighbours a couple of pixels away. Their bodies fly across the map from t = 0, and you can watch the neighbours stay together, then split. The map keeps its own time while they fly.

A body counts as escaped once it is at least 3 units and 4 pair-separations from the other two, moving away from them, and no longer bound to them. The ring above A and B marks the equilateral start, which falls straight into a triple collision.

The GPU works in single precision with a regularized leapfrog integrator (logarithmic Hamiltonian), which steps cleanly through close encounters. In chaotic zones single pixels are not reproducible, but the structure is. Tapped orbits are recomputed in double precision.
